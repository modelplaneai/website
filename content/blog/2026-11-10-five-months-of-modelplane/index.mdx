We open sourced Modelplane on June 23. Less than five months later, we've shipped
[six releases](https://github.com/modelplaneai/modelplane/releases), welcomed
contributions from people outside the original team, started running our own
engineers' coding agents and security workflows on the project, and brought the
people building the inference stack together for Inference Day. This week, from
the KubeCon keynote stage, Bassam shared another milestone: Upbound has submitted
Modelplane to the CNCF for consideration as a Sandbox project.

The submission matters, but what makes this moment worth celebrating is the work
that led here. Models, serving engines, accelerators and infrastructure providers
are evolving independently. Managing them together as one heterogeneous fleet is
becoming a job of its own. We wanted to help build the control plane for that job,
in the open, with the people who will actually use and extend it.

The industry is moving horizontally for practical reasons, not because GPUs have
become cheap or plentiful. Capacity is constrained, models have very different
economics and capabilities, and the best choice for one workload may be completely
wrong for another. Enterprises need to bring their existing cloud commitments,
neocloud capacity and private infrastructure together without being locked into
one model, engine or accelerator. A fleet-wide control plane can help make those
choices usable. That was the idea behind Modelplane's first release, and it's
shaped the work we've done since.

The first release gave teams a way to provision or bring their own inference
clusters, schedule model replicas onto compatible GPUs, cache model weights and
serve requests through one OpenAI-compatible endpoint. It was an ambitious
starting point, but it gave us something real to build on. In July, v0.2 added
Azure AKS and Nebius support alongside weighted routing and controls for reserving
or draining capacity. In August, v0.3 brought Vultr VKE, end-to-end support for
the Anthropic Messages API, including coding-agent workflows, and local testing
across two clusters without needing cloud infrastructure or a GPU.

September's v0.4 grew out of collaboration with the NVIDIA team, adding a Dynamo
serving stack with Grove, the KAI Scheduler and ModelExpress, as well as NVIDIA AI
Cluster Runtime integration. October's v0.5 strengthened the day-to-day operating
story with an AI gateway, regional gateway placement, fleet-wide telemetry,
scale-to-zero and support for Civo. Then v0.6, released on October 30, tackled
some particularly practical limits: scheduling on free GPUs rather than whole
nodes so smaller models can share hardware, support for AMD Instinct and CPU
serving, and more flexible SGLang prefill/decode configurations. None of these
releases is the finish line; together, they show how quickly the project is
beginning to accommodate a broader inference ecosystem.

The work hasn't been confined to the original maintainers. The release notes have
welcomed first-time contributors, including [@adamwg](https://github.com/adamwg)
and [@ytsarev](https://github.com/ytsarev) earlier in the project and
[@RedbackThomson](https://github.com/RedbackThomson) and
[@jbw976](https://github.com/jbw976) in v0.6. Their changes range from making
development and documentation more approachable to improving the underlying
platform. Those contributions matter because an open control plane only becomes
useful to a wider ecosystem when people outside the original design conversation
can shape it.

On November 5, we hosted [Inference Day](https://modelplane.ai/inference-day) to
bring the people building the inference stack into one conversation. Kelsey
Hightower opened with a fireside chat about what platform teams should build
now—and what lessons from cloud native are worth carrying forward. The serving
panel brought maintainers from NVIDIA Dynamo, KServe, Envoy AI Gateway and
Modelplane together around the path from a request to a running engine. Another
connected Kubernetes, Metal3, Crossplane and Modelplane contributors around the
infrastructure underneath models, from clouds to bare metal. A third focused on
the practical work of operating inference: observability, cost and reliability.
Viktor Farcic closed with Inference 101.

That lineup made the horizontal ecosystem story real. No one project can or should
own every layer, and platform engineers need to understand how the pieces
interact. We also published
[An Open Field Guide to Inference Engineering](https://docs.modelplane.ai/guides/inference-guide/),
drawing on the work of the wider community and our own experiments. It walks
through the familiar skills—scheduling, capacity, load balancing and incident
response—and what changes when the workload is inference.

## Champagne: what happened when we became our own users

One of the most important things we did was admit an uncomfortable truth: we were
building technology for self-hosted inference, but we weren't depending on it
ourselves. Project Champagne changed that. We put internal chat, coding agents and
a build-security agent onto models served through Modelplane and asked our
engineers to use them for real work. We also made a rule: don't hide an issue in a
private fork. Work around it temporarily if necessary, but file it, fix it upstream
and write down what happened.

That decision exposed problems before we even got to model quality. An approved GPU
quota didn't mean the instance was available; our first-choice H100 shape never
launched, and another GPU shape was unavailable in several zones. A response could
return HTTP 200 after being silently truncated. A long-running agent could outlive
the credentials or timeouts set up for an ordinary request. We learned that the
environment around a model mattered at least as much as the model itself.

![One fleet control plane — provisioning, scheduling, autoscaling, caching and routing — over TPU, NVIDIA and AMD accelerators across clouds and providers.](/blog/five-months-of-modelplane/heterogeneous-fleet.png)

That experience has reinforced why we've focused on operating a heterogeneous fleet
rather than one ideal cluster. At KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America, Bassam
demonstrated the same idea from another angle: the same model running on NVIDIA
GPUs in AWS and AMD GPUs on Vultr, with different serving engines behind one
endpoint. The infrastructure and serving technology could change while the
application-facing interface stayed consistent. It's a demonstration of the
fleet-wide operating model we're building—and the kind of flexibility that matters
when available capacity and workload needs keep changing.

![The KubeCon demo: one model served from NVIDIA GPUs on AWS and AMD GPUs on Vultr, with vLLM and SGLang behind a single endpoint.](/blog/five-months-of-modelplane/serve-two-deployments.png)

The most revealing failure looked exactly like a bad model. For roughly two weeks,
a coding model mangled tool names, omitted fields and produced nonsense that made
our engineers question whether open models were ready. Then we asked it to copy
back 50 names. With vLLM it succeeded 30% of the time; with SGLang, using the same
weights on the same GPUs, it succeeded 100%. We hadn't changed the intelligence.
We'd changed the software serving it.

The build-security agent taught us a different kind of caution. Open models could
find known CVEs, but a convincing-looking fix plan could still recommend the wrong
patched version. In other cases the evaluation harness, a timeout or the grader—not
the model—explained a disappointing score. If we were going to use self-hosted
inference for real work, we needed to test the whole system rather than infer
quality from a single answer.

And getting a useful pilot wasn't the same as serving a team. About ten engineers
were trying a self-hosted coding model. We tested seven serving recipes to
understand what it would take to support fifteen, changing one variable at a time.
Two GPU nodes couldn't meet the test; four nodes using SGLang could, at roughly
what we were already paying per engineer. One surprise: supposedly light users
could generate more concurrent requests than heavy users because their agents
spawned subagents.

These lessons have guided very practical upstream fixes: gateway TLS and API keys,
configurable request and idle timeouts, OpenTelemetry collection, scale-to-zero and
more useful serving metrics. Not every issue is solved, and we've been explicit
about the ones still open. We published the complete six-part Champagne series
because our own code isn't the only thing that should improve. Other engineers
should be able to inspect the experiments, question the results and avoid repeating
our mistakes.

That's why the CNCF submission feels like a continuation of the work, not a separate
announcement. We've learned from contributors, integration partners, the people who
came to Inference Day and engineers willing to use an unfinished system in their
daily work. The values behind Champagne—use what you build, share the failures and
bring the fixes back—are the same ones we want to carry into a broader community.
Modelplane is still Apache 2.0 licensed and still early. We've submitted it for
consideration as a CNCF Sandbox project; acceptance has not yet been decided. A
neutral home would give people building models, engines, accelerators and
infrastructure a stronger place to shape a common fleet-wide control plane together.

## Join us for what's next

We're proud of the six releases, new infrastructure and serving integrations, and
contributions from outside the original team. We're equally proud of the untidy
parts: GPU requests that failed, serving configurations that lost, broken tool
calls and experiments that changed our minds. They're the parts worth sharing
because they make a young project more useful. If you're building any part of the
inference stack—or suddenly finding yourself responsible for operating it—we'd love
to compare notes, learn from what you've seen and build the next part together.

The six-part Champagne series tells the longer engineering story, including the
things that didn't fit into a keynote demo:

- **Part 1 — [What It Takes to Own Your Intelligence](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-what-it-takes-to-own-your-intelligence):** why we moved our own workloads.
- **Part 2 — [Finding the Corks](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-part-2-finding-the-corks):** capacity, gateways, authentication and the failures that looked healthy.
- **Part 3 — [The Build Security Agent](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-part-3-use-case-1-the-build-security-agent):** evaluating self-hosted models against a real CVE workflow and learning when to distrust the grader.
- **Part 4 — [Candling the Bottles](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-part-4-candling-the-bottles):** building observability that exposes the failures status fields can hide.
- **Part 5 — [Growing a Self-Hosted Coding Model From Pilot to Team](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-part-5-what-does-it-take-to-grow-a-self-hosted-coding-model-from-pilot-to-team):** testing concurrency and the user experience before expanding adoption.
- **Part 6 — [The Model Was Rarely the Problem](https://www.upbound.io/blog/drinking-our-own-champagne-part-6-the-model-was-rarely-the-problem):** the operational lessons and improvements flowing back into Modelplane.

If you've hit a strange failure in your own inference setup, bring it to the
community. The project is still young, and there's a lot more we can learn in
public.
